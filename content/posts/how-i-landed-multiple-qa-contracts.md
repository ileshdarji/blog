Two years ago I was a permanent employee wondering if the day-rate world was for people like me.

Today I'm running concurrent contracts at two major public sector organisations, billing more than I ever earned in permanent employment, and choosing which projects I take on rather than hoping the next one would be interesting.

This is not a success story with a tidy arc. It's a real account of what happened — including the months where nothing happened.

## Why I Made the Move

The decision wasn't financial, even though the money is significantly better. It was about leverage.

In permanent roles, my career moved at the pace of performance review cycles and organisational headcount decisions. As a contractor, I move at the pace of the market. If a contract ends or becomes unsuitable, I find another. My progression is determined by the quality of my work and my reputation, not by whether my manager had budget for a promotion.

That felt right.

## What Didn't Work At First

I spent the first three months refreshing job boards, applying to every QA contract I saw, and getting almost no responses.

The problem was my CV. It was written for a permanent role. It described my responsibilities. Nobody hiring a contractor wants to know your responsibilities — they want to know what problems you solve.

Compare these two descriptions of the same role:

**Before:** *"Responsible for manual and automated testing of web applications using Selenium and Cypress."*

**After:** *"Built a Playwright automation framework from scratch that reduced regression suite execution time from 45 minutes to 8 minutes, enabling daily releases instead of weekly ones."*

The second version answers the question a hiring manager is asking: "What will this person actually do for us?"

## Building the Right CV

A contractor CV has three jobs:
1. Get past keyword filters (ATS systems are aggressive)
2. Make a technical lead want to call you
3. Survive a 30-second scan by a recruiter who doesn't understand QA

For keywords, I used every variation of the tools and techniques I knew: Playwright, TypeScript, Selenium, Cypress, API testing, Postman, pytest, CI/CD, GitHub Actions, BDD, test automation, SDET. Spelled out in full, not just acronyms.

For impact statements, every bullet point had to answer either "what changed?" or "why does this matter?" If I couldn't answer those questions, I deleted the bullet.

For scannability, I added a technology summary table at the top. Three columns: Tools, Languages, Methodologies. Recruiters can scan it in 5 seconds and know whether to call me.

## LinkedIn Did More Work Than Any Job Board

The contracts I'm most proud of didn't come from job boards. They came from recruiters finding me on LinkedIn.

My profile used to say: *"Senior SDET | Automation | Selenium | Cypress"*

It now says: *"QA Contractor | Playwright · Typescript · API Testing | Helping teams build fast, reliable automation at scale"*

The difference isn't cosmetic. The first version describes what I am. The second version describes what I do for clients.

Beyond the headline, I post consistently. Three times a week. Real QA content — not motivational posts, not "5 reasons to be happy at work." Actual posts like:

- The biggest Playwright mistake I see teams making
- Why AI-generated tests fail in production
- What I learned building test frameworks at Home Office

Each post is 150–300 words. Three to five ideas. Ends with a clear point. Takes 20 minutes to write.

The effect was gradual and then sudden. For three months, my posts got 50–100 views. Then one post on Playwright fixtures got 3,000 views and eight recruiter messages in two days.

Consistency compounds.

## The Rate Conversation

New contractors consistently undercharge. I did too.

The rule I follow now: find out what the market is paying (Technojobs, JobServe, asking other contractors directly — yes, we do this), then start at the upper end of that range.

The reason most people undercharge is fear. Fear that they'll price themselves out of opportunities. In practice, the opposite is true. A rate that's too low signals risk to a discerning client. A confident rate signals confidence.

You can always negotiate down slightly. You cannot easily negotiate up from a low opening.

For inside IR35 vs outside IR35: understand the difference before you quote a rate. They're not comparable without adjustment. I've seen contractors take inside IR35 roles at outside IR35 rates and wonder why their take-home was much lower than expected.

## Managing Two Contracts Simultaneously

This is possible but requires discipline.

Both clients need to feel like your primary focus. The way I do this is by delivering clearly and communicating consistently. Weekly written updates, even when nothing dramatic happened. Blockers surfaced early, not the night before a deadline. Documentation written as I go, not retroactively.

The biggest risk when running multiple contracts is task-switching. Context switching between codebases, teams, and domains costs more than most people expect. I keep strict time boundaries and don't mix work across contexts in the same hour.

## What I'd Do From Day One

If I were starting again:

1. **Fix the CV first.** Impact over responsibilities. Every bullet.
2. **Start posting on LinkedIn before I needed it.** Build the audience before the job search.
3. **Talk to other contractors immediately.** The market rate, the gotchas, the good agencies — other contractors will tell you these things freely.
4. **Treat every contract as a reference.** Your reputation is your pipeline. One bad contract exit can cost you more than a month's billing in lost opportunities.
5. **Learn the IR35 rules properly.** Not the summary version. The actual rules.

The contracting market for experienced QA engineers is strong and getting stronger. The skills that make you valuable — test strategy, framework design, mentoring teams, working with stakeholders — are skills that are hard to automate and high in demand.

The barrier isn't skill. For most people, it's just the decision to start.
